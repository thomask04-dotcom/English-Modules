Print Conventions

1. Print Conventions: What They Are and Why They Matter

NESA describes print conventions as understandings about how print works in texts and the environment. Knowing how print works in English is an essential part of learning to read and write. In the NSW English K–10 Syllabus (2022), Print conventions is a focus area with one outcome, in Early Stage 1. Researchers describe concepts about print as a constrained skill, one that can be mastered in a relatively short time (Paris 2005). In the Reading Rope, print concepts are part of Literacy Knowledge, a strand of Language Comprehension (Scarborough 2001).

Two Kinds of Print Knowledge

Features of print

What print is made of, and what it does: print carries a message, written Standard Australian English uses letters to represent sounds, and texts contain letters, words, spaces, punctuation, numerals, pictures and symbols.

Directionality of print

How to move through print: holding a text the right way up, finding the front and back of a book, turning pages one at a time, and reading from top to bottom and left to right, with a return sweep to each new line.

Common Misconceptions

Students will pick it up from being read to

NESA describes the explicit and systematic teaching of print conventions as the deliberate explanation and demonstration of how print works, in a planned and sequential way. It is an important part of early literacy learning that should not be overlooked.

It needs teaching again every year

Print conventions is a constrained body of knowledge. NESA notes that once students have developed an understanding of print conventions, further teaching is not required. The task is to find out what each student already knows and to teach what is missing.

Summarised from NESA (2022), English K–10 Syllabus support: Teaching advice for Print conventions (Early Stage 1) and Reading comprehension (Stages 2 and 3), and the syllabus content for Print conventions.

Key message: print conventions are how print works: what it is made of, and how to move through it. Teach them explicitly and early, check what each student knows, and stop once they are secure.

3. How Learning Is Organised Within the Stage

Features of print: knowing

All 10 points in Features of print begin with understand, identify, know, distinguish or recognise. They name knowledge to teach explicitly: what letters, words, spaces, punctuation, numerals and symbols are, and that print carries a message.

Directionality of print: doing

All 6 points in Directionality of print are actions students perform with a text: orient it, find the front and back, turn pages, start at the top, track left to right with a return sweep, and read the left page first. These are practised every time a text is shared or read.

4. Pedagogy: The Evidence Base

This step sets out what the evidence base of the syllabus says about teaching print conventions: NESA's teaching advice, and the research behind it.

What the Evidence Base Says

Explicit teaching: every student's entitlement

NESA describes the explicit and systematic teaching of print conventions as the deliberate explanation and demonstration of how print works, in a planned and sequential way. Instruction for print conventions is an important component of early literacy learning that should not be overlooked.

Students start school with varying levels of knowledge about print. The syllabus content for Print conventions is part of every student's learning entitlement, and explicit teaching guarantees equity: it gives every student, not only those who have been read to often, the knowledge they need to begin reading and writing.

Summarised from NESA (2022), English K–10 Syllabus support: Teaching advice for Print conventions (Early Stage 1). Describing the syllabus content as a learning entitlement is this module's framing.

NESA advises that instruction for print conventions should focus on showing students:

1. What the conventions are

  • Explain and demonstrate each convention deliberately: what a letter, a word, a space and a full stop are, and where reading starts.

In practice: use a big book or enlarged text so every student can see the print, and name the convention as you point to it.

2. How they are used in print-based texts

  • Show how the same conventions appear across a range of print-based texts.

In practice: look at the same features in storybooks, information texts, signs, labels and environmental print around the school.

3. How to apply them in reading

  • Show how to apply the conventions to help fluent reading and comprehension.

In practice: during shared reading, track under each word as you read, model the return sweep, and pause at punctuation.

4. How to apply them in writing

  • Show how to apply the conventions when creating their own texts.

In practice: in modelled and shared writing, think aloud about where to start, leaving a space between words, and starting a sentence with a capital letter.

Supporting EAL/D students. EAL/D students may have developed ideas about how print works from their first language, which may use different conventions. Identifying a student's proficiency in the Writing modes of the EAL/D Learning Progression can help teachers understand their needs, and the ESL Scales for Writing level 1 can support their English language learning in this focus area. EAL/D students who enter school in Years 3–6 may still be learning basic features of English print, such as directionality.
Students with hearing or vision needs. NESA provides additional teaching advice on hearing and vision support in the primary curriculum.

Summarised from NESA (2022), English K–10 Syllabus support: Teaching advice for Print conventions (Early Stage 1) and Reading comprehension (Stages 2 and 3). Points marked In practice are this module's elaboration.

What the Research Adds

Drawing attention to print during shared reading

In a randomised controlled trial, teachers who referred to print during shared reading, by talking about it and by pointing to it, improved young children's later reading, spelling and comprehension, with effects lasting two years (Piasta et al. 2012). Small changes to how a book is read aloud can make print visible to students.

Necessary, but not sufficient

Paris (2005) argues that constrained skills such as concepts about print are mastered quickly and are necessary for reading, but are not on their own a cause of reading success. Teach them well and early, then move on, while vocabulary and comprehension keep growing.

Key message: teach print conventions deliberately, in a planned sequence, through shared reading and modelled writing, and make sure every student applies them in their own reading and writing.

5. Teaching Advice

This tab draws out the essential knowledge for teachers from NESA's teaching advice for Print conventions, stage by stage. Quoted text is NESA's wording. It is followed by what NESA's additional teaching advice adds.

Essential Knowledge from NESA's Teaching Advice

☞ Select a stage to see NESA's essential advice for that stage.

What print conventions are

“Print conventions are understandings about how print works in texts and the environment. Knowing how print works in English is an essential part of learning to read and write.” Skilled readers and writers use them to “efficiently navigate their way around a text when reading” and to “create written texts with spaces between words.”

Teach what students need

“Students start school with varying levels of knowledge about print conventions in English.” “Once students have developed an understanding of print conventions, further teaching is not required.”

Explicit and systematic

“Explicit and systematic teaching of print conventions involves the deliberate explanation and demonstration of how print works in a planned and sequential way.” Instruction “is an important component of early literacy learning that should not be overlooked.”

It should focus on showing students:

  • what these conventional patterns are
  • how they are used in a range of print-based texts
  • how to apply them to help fluent reading and comprehension
  • how to apply them in creating their own texts.

EAL/D learners

EAL/D students “may have already developed ideas about how print works based on their first language. Languages use different print conventions.” For example, “In Arabic, print travels from right to left across the page.”

Print conventions has no Stage 1 focus area. Students who have not yet developed these understandings continue to need explicit teaching, as in Early Stage 1.

Print conventions has no Stage 2 or 3 focus area. NESA notes that EAL/D students who arrive in Years 3–6:

New arrivals

“may still be learning the alphabet, left to right directionality, the conventions of English print and letter–sound relationships.” (Reading fluency advice) Students with limited literacy background “may be unfamiliar with books and require explicit teaching of directionality, print marks and English print conventions.” (Understanding and responding to literature advice)

Early Stage 1 and Stage 1: NESA, Teaching advice: Early Stage 1 – English and Teaching advice: Stage 1 – English. Stages 2 and 3: NESA, English K–10 Syllabus: Teaching advice (Early Stage 1 to Stage 3). Stage 3 advice matches Stage 2 except where noted.

What NESA's Additional Teaching Advice Adds

NESA's additional teaching advice for Phonological awareness includes a strategy that links spoken language to print conventions. Quoted text is NESA's wording.

Line writing

“Being able to identify spoken word boundaries is an important prerequisite for developing print conventions. Line writing is a teaching strategy that offers students a visual scaffold for understanding both spoken and written word boundaries. It can provide a bridge between awareness of phonology and print conventions.”

The teacher draws a line for each word as a sentence is spoken. For example, a student says ‘The dog barked’ and the teacher draws 3 lines, then points to each line as the student repeats the sentence. Students can also jump into a hoop for each spoken word.

The essential elements in print conventions teaching

Agency

Learners make choices, set goals and take actions that influence their learning.

In print conventions: students choose books, hold and read them independently, and check their own writing for spaces and starting points.

Environment

The tangible and intangible realities that enable each individual to flourish within a connected community.

In print conventions: a print-rich classroom, with labels, names, signs and texts at students' eye level, and texts in students' home languages.

Equity

Inclusive, high-quality opportunities, resources and support for every student.

In print conventions: explicit teaching for students who start school with less experience of print, and support for EAL/D students whose first language works differently.

Relevance

Connecting learners' goals, needs and interests with real-world experiences that are meaningful and purposeful.

In print conventions: students' names, personally significant words and the environmental print they see every day.

Rigour

The depth, thoroughness and challenging nature of an educational experience.

In print conventions: precise language for each feature, and checking that every student can apply each convention, not just recognise it.

7. Assessment: Finding Out What Students Know About Print

Because students start school with varying knowledge of print, and because further teaching is not required once a convention is understood, assessment tells you who needs teaching, and what. NESA's teaching advice for Print conventions doesn't set out assessment methods, though it points teachers to the Writing modes of the EAL/D Learning Progression and the ESL Scales for Writing level 1 for EAL/D students. This step adds practical suggestions.

What to Assess

AreaThe question it answersExample task
Book handlingDoes the student know how a book works?Hand the student a book upside down and back to front. “Show me the front of the book.” “Where do I start reading?” “Which page do I read first?”
Tracking printDoes the student move through print correctly?“Point to the words as I read.” Watch for left to right, return sweep and top to bottom.
Features of printCan the student tell letters, words, spaces, punctuation and numerals apart?“Show me one letter. Now show me one word.” “Find a space.” “What is this?” (pointing to a full stop or a numeral).
Upper- and lower-case lettersCan the student identify and name both forms?“Find a capital letter.” “What's the name of this letter?”
Print in writingDoes the student apply conventions when writing?Look at a writing sample for left-to-right direction, top-to-bottom order and spaces between words.

The areas follow the syllabus content for ENE-PRINT-01. The questions and tasks are this module's suggestions.

Ways to Assess Print Conventions

A one-to-one book check

  • Sit with each student and a picture book, and work through a short set of book-handling and print questions.
  • Record what each student can show, so you know who needs teaching.

Observation in shared reading

  • Invite students to point to words, find the first word on a page, or show where to go next.
  • Note who responds confidently and who follows others.

Writing samples

  • Look at directionality, spacing and the use of letters rather than other marks.
  • Writing shows whether students apply conventions without prompting.

EAL/D learners in any year

  • Check print conventions for EAL/D students who enter school in any year, as their first language may use different conventions.
  • Use the EAL/D Learning Progression and the ESL Scales to understand their needs.

Assessing the Content

Assessment works best when it is tied to the content students are learning. For example:

Content point (NESA)Assessment idea
Know the difference between a letter and a wordGive the student two small cards to frame “just one letter”, then “just one word”, in a familiar text.
Identify spaces between wordsAsk the student to count the words in a short sentence by pointing to each one, and show where the spaces are.
Track text left to right and use return sweepWhile you read a two-line sentence aloud, the student points under each word. Watch for the return sweep to the second line.
Consistently read left page before right pageOn a double-page spread, ask “Which page do I read first?” across several books.
Recognise symbols, icons and personally significant words in everyday situations and in textsShow the student their own name, familiar signs and icons, and ask what they say or mean.

Principles for Assessing Print Conventions

  • Assess early. Check what each student knows when they start school, so teaching starts where they are.
  • Assess individually. Group responses can hide students who are copying others.
  • Assess application, not just recognition. Check that students use conventions when they read and write.
  • Stop when it is secure. Once a student understands a convention, further teaching is not required.
  • Check again for new arrivals. Students who join later, including EAL/D students in any year, may need assessing.
  • Use the evidence. Feed what you learn into the next OCI cycle (Step 6): who needs which convention taught, and who is ready to move on.

Key message: assess print conventions early and individually, teach only what each student is missing, and check again for students who arrive later.
