Literacy and English: Understanding the Difference

1. Literacy and English: What's the Difference?

Literacy and English are closely connected, but they are not the same thing. Being clear about the difference helps teachers plan for both: the systematic teaching of foundational skills, and the rich, conceptual study of language and literature.

Literacy

Literacy is the fundamental capability to interpret, evaluate and create meaning across a diverse range of oral, print, visual and digital texts. Its core knowledge and skills are explicitly developed within the English learning area, but literacy is a dynamic and flexible practice reinforced across all disciplines. It involves the purposeful use of language (listening, speaking, reading, viewing and writing) to learn effectively, communicate confidently and participate productively in society.

English

English is the study of language and literature through which students learn to interact with the world. The 2022 syllabus takes a conceptual approach, moving from foundational “code-breaking” in the early years to sophisticated critical analysis in later stages. English is not just about reading: it is the study of texts with enduring value, and it develops confident communicators, critical and imaginative thinkers, and lifelong learners.

NESA, English K–10 Syllabus (2022), overview“The organisation of the syllabus supports the development of early literacy knowledge and skills, while continuing to acknowledge the importance of learning about and enjoying literature.”

What the Rationale Says

The syllabus rationale names both. One paragraph is about literacy; the next is about English.

“Language and text shape our understanding of ourselves and our world. This allows us to relate with others, and contributes to our intellectual, social and emotional development. In English K–10, students study language in its various textual forms, which develop in complexity, to understand how meaning is shaped, conveyed, interpreted, and reflected.”

“Students engage with literature from Australia, including the rich voices of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples, and from across the world. These texts communicate in distinctive ways and are shaped by lived experiences, knowledge, cultures, and connections. By exploring historic and contemporary texts, representative of a range of cultural and social perspectives, students broaden their experiences and become empowered to express their identities, personal values and ethics.”

Literacy“Students develop foundational literacy skills in the early years and progressively build on these skills. This enables them to learn about and control language in a range of increasingly sophisticated contexts.”

English“Through interrelated practices and experiences in understanding and creating texts, students learn about the power, purpose, value and art of English. The development of these interconnected skills and understandings supports students to become confident communicators, critical and imaginative thinkers, and informed and active participants in society.”

NESA, English K–10 Syllabus (2022), Rationale (updated with the release of the 3–10 outcomes and content).

Literature: The Heart of English

NESA, English K–10 Syllabus (2022)“Literature is defined as a body of work that has enduring personal, social, cultural or aesthetic value. It comprises a dynamic and evolving range of fiction and nonfiction texts from diverse contemporary, historical and cultural contexts.”

Key message: literacy is the capability to make and interpret meaning, built on foundational skills and used in every subject. English is the discipline: the study of language and literature that develops that capability and goes far beyond it.

2. Literacy: The Foundational Skills

Early literacy skills and knowledge form the foundation for learning in English. These foundational skills are interrelated and often develop in parallel. In the syllabus they are taught through these focus areas:

Phonological awarenessPrint conventions (Early Stage 1)Phonic knowledgeSpellingHandwritingReading fluency

Most of these are constrained skills: there is a finite set of knowledge to master, such as the letter–sound correspondences or letter formations, and students can master them completely (Paris, 2005).

Phonological awareness and phonic knowledge

Support understanding of the alphabetic code, to develop decoding (reading) and encoding (spelling) skills.

Handwriting and spelling

The transcription skills that support writing fluency. When they are automatic, they reduce cognitive load, freeing attention for composing.

Reading fluency

Automaticity and prosody form the bridge from word recognition to reading comprehension (Rasinski, 2012). NESA describes fluency as “the bridge from phonics to comprehension”.

Print conventions (Early Stage 1)

Taught in Early Stage 1. Understandings about how print works, which once mastered need no further teaching. NESA: “Once students have developed an understanding of print conventions, further teaching is not required.”

How Foundational Skills Are Taught

Teaching for foundational skills is systematic, cumulative and explicit, including teaching students to understand and use the alphabetic code. It should:

Interrelated approach

The focus areas of Phonological awareness, Phonic knowledge, Spelling, Handwriting and Reading fluency, with Print conventions in Early Stage 1, are taught in parallel, in an interrelated way, rather than one after another. Learning in one focus area strengthens and is applied in the others.

Use whole class, small group and targeted teaching

Explicit whole-class teaching, then small-group and targeted instruction informed by assessment, so every student masters the skills.

Use aligned resources

All resources must align to both the syllabus and the instructional sequence, so students have authentic and purposeful opportunities to practise and apply what they have been taught. For example, decodable texts, magnetic letters, sentence strips and Elkonin boxes are used only with the GPCs and skills already taught. See the Phonic Knowledge and Spelling modules for the CEDoW instructional models.

NESA, Additional teaching advice, Phonic knowledge, Early Stage 1“In Early Stage 1 it is essential to teach the initial phonics code: explicitly, systematically, sequentially, cumulatively.”

Literacy Across the Curriculum

Literacy is not confined to English. Students use their literacy to learn in every subject, and every subject has its own literacy demands, from reading a science procedure to writing a historical recount.

NESA, NSW Curriculum, Capabilities and priorities“Applying literacy knowledge and skills across the curriculum can enrich the study of all learning areas and help students develop a broader and deeper understanding of literacy.”

Key message: foundational literacy skills are constrained, masterable and taught systematically, cumulatively and explicitly, so that every student can access the rest of English and the whole curriculum.

3. English: The Discipline

English develops deep, discipline-specific knowledge and understanding through explicit teaching of knowledge and skill, alongside purposeful and intentional opportunities to explore, analyse, experiment with and enjoy quality literature through the study of English textual concepts. It is taught through these focus areas:

Understanding and responding to literatureReading comprehensionCreating written textsVocabularyOral language and communication

Oral language and communication forms a solid foundation for communication and underpins all learning, in both foundational skills and English. These focus areas are unconstrained: knowledge of vocabulary, comprehension and literature keeps growing throughout life (Paris, 2005).

Oral language and communication

Talk about texts, ideas and learning: discussing literature, building vocabulary in use, planning writing orally and presenting to audiences.

Reading comprehension and Vocabulary

Developed by listening to, reading and responding to literature, as well as through explicit teaching.

Understanding and responding to literature

Provides opportunities to think critically about how meaning is shaped, conveyed, interpreted and reflected in texts.

Creating written texts

Provides opportunities for planning, creating and revising texts for different purposes and audiences.

A Conceptual Approach

The syllabus takes a conceptual approach to English, through a focus on textual concepts and quality literature. In K–6, the Understanding and responding to literature content is organised around concepts such as context, narrative, character, imagery, symbol and connotation, perspective, argument, genre and theme. Concepts act as lenses for studying literature, so students move beyond basic comprehension to analyse, experiment with and appreciate increasingly complex texts.

NESA, Teaching advice, Understanding and responding to literature, Stages 2 and 3Concepts in texts “can be used as lenses through which to study literature.” “Text selection for the instructional purpose is important. For example, a poem may lend itself to teaching the concepts of imagery, symbol and connotation, while an advertisement or speech may be suitable for teaching the concepts of argument and authority.”

The NSW Department of Education's English textual concepts resources, developed with the English Teachers Association NSW, support teachers to plan for these concepts.

How English Is Taught

Explicit teaching through gradual release

Teachers model, then share and guide, then students work independently: I do, we do, you do (Pearson and Gallagher, 1983). NESA describes a cycle of “modelled teaching, shared teaching, guided teaching and independent learning” for writing in Years 3–6.

Quality literature and multimodal texts

Resources include quality literature, multimodal texts, and planning and writing scaffolds. “Students benefit from their teacher reading literature to them that is more complex in structure, language and visual elements than the texts they may read independently.”

Rich, purposeful writing

Embeds rich and purposeful opportunities for students to plan, compose and revise written texts for a range of purposes and audiences.

Meaningful connections

Supports meaningful connections between syllabus focus areas, outcomes and content, so students apply what they learn in one focus area to another.

Key message: English is a discipline with its own concepts and body of knowledge. It combines explicit teaching with the intentional study and enjoyment of quality literature.

4. How Literacy and English Work Together

Literacy and English are not two separate programs competing for time. Foundational skills give students access to texts; the study of English gives those skills purpose and depth. Both are needed from the first day of school.

Foundational skills (literacy)

Phonological awarenessPrint conventions (Early Stage 1)Phonic knowledgeSpellingHandwritingReading fluency

  • Constrained: finite and masterable
  • Systematic, cumulative and explicit
  • Whole class, small group and targeted
  • Resources aligned to the syllabus and instructional sequence
  • Goal: accuracy and automaticity

Conceptual understandings (English)

Understanding and responding to literatureReading comprehensionCreating written textsVocabulary

  • Unconstrained: grow throughout life
  • Explicit teaching through gradual release, and textual concepts
  • Whole class, with guided and independent work
  • Quality literature, multimodal texts, planning and writing scaffolds
  • Goal: deep understanding, critical and creative thinking

Oral language and communication underpins both: it forms a solid foundation for communication and underpins all learning.

NESA, English K–10 Syllabus (2022), overview“Many connections exist between the focus areas in English. Knowledge of and skills for focus areas often develop in an interrelated manner and are therefore best addressed in parallel.” “Focus areas should not be interpreted as hierarchical or time bound, as instructional priorities will be informed by learner needs. Across a stage of learning all focus areas need to be taught.”

5. Planning with Purpose

Planning begins with the purpose of instruction. Is the lesson to explicitly teach foundational literacy knowledge and skills, or to teach subject-specific knowledge in English? That purpose then influences every other planning decision:

  • text selection
  • the sequence of learning
  • the use of High Impact Teaching Strategies (HITS)
  • resource selection
  • evidence-based teaching practices
  • the opportunities students have to transfer and apply their learning
  • what mastery looks like

Planning and Programming

Programming foundational skills

Plan dedicated, systematic lessons for the constrained skills, following an instructional sequence and grouping students by assessed need. Oral language and communication underpins this learning.

Programming English

Plan units around quality literature and textual concepts, combining explicit teaching with exploration, analysis and experimentation, and connecting Reading comprehension, Vocabulary, Understanding and responding to literature, Creating written texts, and Oral language and communication.

Key message: start with purpose. Whether you are explicitly teaching foundational literacy or subject-specific knowledge in English determines your texts, sequence, HITS, resources, teaching practices, opportunities to transfer and apply, and what mastery looks like.
