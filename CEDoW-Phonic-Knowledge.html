Phonic Knowledge

1. Phonic Knowledge: What It Is and Why It Matters

NESA describes phonic knowledge as the relationship between speech sounds (phonemes) and their letter symbols (graphemes). Understanding and using the English alphabetic code is essential for fluent and meaningful reading and writing in English. In the NSW English K–10 Syllabus (2022), Phonic knowledge is a focus area with outcomes in Early Stage 1 and Stage 1. In the Reading Rope, it is part of Decoding, a strand of Word Recognition (Scarborough 2001), and in the Writing Rope it underpins spelling, part of Transcription (Sedita 2019).

One Code, Two Directions

NESA states that phonic knowledge is demonstrated when students can accurately decode words that they read, by blending phonemes, and accurately encode (spell) words that they write, by segmenting phonemes.

The initial phonics code

Frequently occurring grapheme–phoneme correspondences (GPCs): single-letter graphemes and consonant digraphs (e.g. sh, th, ch, ck, ng), plus frequently occurring split digraphs (e.g. a-e, o-e) and some vowel digraphs (e.g. oy, ay). This is the focus of Kindergarten.

The extended phonics code

The broader range of graphemes for each phoneme, such as the graphemes for r-controlled vowels (ur in hurt, ir in bird, er in her). Students consolidate the initial code and master the extended code in Stage 1.

Common Misconceptions

Phonics is only about reading

Every content point in Phonic knowledge pairs reading and writing: students blend to decode and segment to encode, and apply both when reading texts and creating texts.

Phonics finishes at the end of Year 2

The outcomes end in Stage 1, but the code keeps working. In Stages 2 and 3, students syllabify, blend GPCs and use morphemic knowledge to read words accurately, and Spelling extends the code with position-dependent graphemes, etymology and infrequent letter patterns.

Key message: phonic knowledge is the alphabetic code that links phonemes and graphemes. Students use it in two directions, blending to read and segmenting to spell, and it must be taught explicitly and systematically.

4. Pedagogy: The Evidence Base

This step sets out what the evidence base of the syllabus says about teaching phonic knowledge: NESA's teaching advice, and the research behind it.

What the Evidence Base Says

Explicit teaching: every student's entitlement

NESA states that in Stage 1, explicit and systematic phonics instruction is essential. Understanding and using the alphabetic code is essential for fluent and meaningful reading and writing in English.

The syllabus content for Phonic knowledge is part of every student's learning entitlement. Explicit, systematic teaching guarantees equity: every student is taught the code, in a planned sequence, rather than left to work it out from exposure to print.

Summarised from NESA (2022), English K–10 Syllabus support: Teaching advice for Phonic knowledge (Stage 1). Describing the syllabus content as a learning entitlement is this module's framing.

NESA's teaching advice has five key messages:

1. Follow a systematic sequence

  • Use the suggested instructional sequences for Early Stage 1 and Stage 1 to inform planning.
  • Early Stage 1: aim to teach 3 to 4 GPCs per week, reducing to 1 to 2 per week as the complexity and scope of graphemes increase (NESA additional teaching advice).
  • Stage 1: aim to explicitly teach 2 to 4 GPCs per week. For some students the rate may need to be slower; others may advance more quickly.

2. Introduce the extended code carefully

  • Once the initial code is mastered, systematically teach 2 to 3 graphemes for each phoneme at a time, beginning with the most common, to avoid cognitive overload.
  • For example, teach ‘or’, ‘aw’, ‘au’ before ‘ore’ and ‘our’. Not every spelling of a phoneme has to be taught in Year 1.

3. Review cumulatively

  • Introduce graphemes cumulatively and review them multiple times across the year, each time using new vocabulary.
  • Begin decoding and encoding with one-syllable words, and gradually progress to multisyllabic words.

4. Practise in decodable texts

  • Choose decodable texts with a clear sequence of GPCs that closely matches the sequence of instruction, and about 80 per cent of the text decodable using taught GPCs.
  • A high lesson-to-text match is more likely to result in immediate success for beginning readers.

5. Move on to natural language texts

  • The use of decodable texts should not be protracted.
  • Once students have mastered all components of the extended code, they should access natural language texts.

Choosing decodable texts

  • Look for texts that systematically introduce high-frequency words, contain connected text with correct grammar and punctuation, and include a story or information that carries meaning.
  • Text should be appropriately sized and well spaced across the page.
Supporting EAL/D students. The home language or dialect of some EAL/D students may not contain certain phonemes present in Standard Australian English, so they may need additional support with unknown phonemes. Some EAL/D learners may benefit from explicit teaching of letter–sound correspondence in Standard Australian English.
Students with hearing or vision needs. NESA provides additional teaching advice on hearing and vision support in the primary curriculum.

Summarised from NESA (2022), English K–10 Syllabus support: Teaching advice for Phonic knowledge (Early Stage 1 and Stage 1) and Spelling (Stage 1).

What the Research Adds

Systematic phonics works

The National Reading Panel's meta-analysis found that systematic phonics instruction helped children learn to read more effectively than instruction with little or no phonics, with the strongest effects when it began early (Ehri, Nunes, Stahl and Willows 2001). Castles, Rastle and Nation (2018) review the wider evidence and conclude that learning the alphabetic code is the essential first step in learning to read.

From decoding to instant recognition

Each time students decode a word, they connect its spelling to its pronunciation and meaning. Through this process, called orthographic mapping, words become stored for instant recognition (Ehri 2014). Phonic knowledge is what makes this mapping possible.

Key message: teach the code explicitly and systematically, following a planned sequence, review it cumulatively, practise it in well-matched decodable texts, and move students to natural language texts once the extended code is mastered.

Essential Knowledge from NESA's Teaching Advice

☞ Select a stage to see NESA's essential advice for that stage.

What phonic knowledge is

“Phonic knowledge refers to the relationship between speech sounds (phonemes) and their letter symbols (graphemes).” “There are 44 unique phonemes in Standard Australian English (SAE). These phonemes are represented by the 26 letters of the alphabet, either individually or in combination.”

How it is demonstrated

Phonic knowledge is demonstrated when students can:

  • accurately decode words that they read, by blending phonemes
  • accurately encode (spell) words that they write, by segmenting phonemes.

The initial phonics code

“The initial phonics code encompasses frequently occurring GPCs, including single-letter graphemes and consonant digraphs (eg sh, th, ch, ck, ng). It also includes frequently occurring split digraphs (eg a-e, o-e) and some of the vowel digraphs (eg oy, ay).”

Decodable texts

“A high lesson-to-text match will more likely result in immediate success for beginning readers.” “The use of decodable texts should not be protracted. Once students have mastered all components of the extended code, they should access natural language texts.” NESA's criteria for selecting decodable texts are in the Instructional Model tab.

EAL/D learners

“The home language spoken by students for whom English is an additional language or dialect (EAL/D) may not contain certain phonemes present in SAE. As such, some of these students may need additional support with unknown phonemes.”

Explicit and systematic

“In Stage 1, explicit and systematic phonics instruction is essential. Students consolidate knowledge of the initial phonics code when decoding (reading) and encoding (spelling) single-syllable words and multisyllabic words.”

The extended code

Students “master the extended phonics code and apply this to a range of reading contexts. This encompasses the broader range of graphemes for each corresponding phoneme.” For example, r-controlled vowels: “ur in words like ‘hurt’, ir in words like ‘bird’ and er in words like ‘her’”.

Pace and sequence

“Aim to explicitly teach 2 to 4 GPCs per week.” “In Stage 1, when students have mastered the initial code, begin systematically teaching 2 to 3 graphemes for each phoneme at a time. To avoid cognitive overload, begin with the most common letter combinations.” “Every spelling of /or/ does not have to be taught in Year 1”.

Cumulative review

“Introduce graphemes cumulatively and review these multiple times across the year, each time using new vocabulary.” “When teaching decoding and encoding skills, begin using one-syllable words and gradually progress to multisyllabic words.”

Phonic knowledge has no Stage 2 or 3 focus area, but the code is still taught where students need it.

Students still learning the code

“In Stage 2 or Stage 3, some students may not have learnt initial and extended phonic knowledge and will need explicit phonics instruction as outlined in Early Stage 1 and Stage 1.” (Syllabus overview) “If a student has not developed automaticity in word reading, it is important to revisit Stage 1 content”. (Reading fluency advice)

Multisyllabic words

“It is important to continue to build students’ decoding and encoding skills so they can read complex multisyllabic words.” In Stage 2, this is addressed “in parallel with Reading fluency as well as in the phonological component of Spelling.” (Reading comprehension advice)

The essential elements in phonic knowledge teaching

Agency

Learners make choices, set goals and take actions that influence their learning.

In phonic knowledge: students use taught strategies, such as blending and segmenting, independently, and track the GPCs they have mastered.

Environment

The tangible and intangible realities that enable each individual to flourish within a connected community.

In phonic knowledge: a sound wall or grapheme chart that grows as GPCs are taught, and a well-organised collection of decodable texts.

Equity

Inclusive, high-quality opportunities, resources and support for every student.

In phonic knowledge: a systematic sequence for every student, with pace adjusted to need and extra support with unknown phonemes for EAL/D students.

Relevance

Connecting learners' goals, needs and interests with real-world experiences that are meaningful and purposeful.

In phonic knowledge: applying taught GPCs straight away to read decodable texts that carry meaning and to write students' own messages.

Rigour

The depth, thoroughness and challenging nature of an educational experience.

In phonic knowledge: mastery of the extended code, accurate decoding and encoding, and the move to natural language texts.

Phonological awareness (Early Stage 1)

Phonological awareness is oral. Of the 16 Early Stage 1 content points, 11 are at the phoneme level. NESA's additional advice notes that “continuous phonemes are generally easier to segment and blend” (for example, m-a-n, s-u-n), and that “Words that contain consonant-vowel beginnings are easier to segment than words containing consonant blends such as ‘stop’.” “Manipulation of phonemes is the most complex phonemic awareness skill.”

NESA, Phonological awareness, Early Stage 1“The phonemic awareness skills of segmenting and blending phonemes are the most critical because they are essential component skills required for reading words (decoding) and spelling words (encoding).”

Blending

“Orally blend up to 4 phonemes together to make a one-syllable spoken word”

Segmenting

“Orally segment one-syllable words comprised of up to 4 phonemes into separate phonemes”

Manipulating

“Blend aloud all phonemes when asked to delete, add or substitute an initial phoneme”, “… a final phoneme”, and “… to substitute a medial vowel phoneme”. For example, sit without /s/ is it.

Independent. Students apply the GPC in words and sentences built from taught GPCs.

Pace and progression

StageNESA advice
Early Stage 1“Aim to teach 3 to 4 GPCs per week. As the complexity and scope of graphemes increase, reduce the pace to 1 to 2 GPCs per week. Some students may require more intensive instruction and consolidated time. Conversely, other students may acquire GPCs more quickly and should be introduced to more complex graphemes sooner.” (Additional teaching advice) “Once students learn how to represent a small group of phonemes using corresponding single-letter graphemes, teach students to use those GPCs to blend and segment a series of phonemes.” Students can then read and write some VC and CVC words, such as at, sat, sap, pat, pan, nap. The initial phonics code “encompasses frequently occurring GPCs, including single-letter graphemes and consonant digraphs (e.g. sh, th, ch, ck, ng). It also includes frequently occurring split digraphs (e.g. a-e, o-e) and some of the vowel digraphs (e.g. oy, ay).” See the Suggested instructional sequence for Early Stage 1: Phonic knowledge.
Stage 1“Aim to explicitly teach 2 to 4 GPCs per week. For some students, this rate may need to be slower to allow sufficient opportunity for consolidation. Other students may be able to advance more quickly.” “When teaching decoding and encoding skills, begin using one-syllable words and gradually progress to multisyllabic words.” Additional advice: consider “explicit teaching multiple times each week”, and “Students’ progress in developing phonics skills should be regularly monitored to ensure individual learning needs are adequately addressed.”

The extended code (Stage 1)

NESA, Phonic knowledge, Stage 1“In Stage 1, when students have mastered the initial code, begin systematically teaching 2 to 3 graphemes for each phoneme at a time. To avoid cognitive overload, begin with the most common letter combinations.” For /or/: “Teach ‘or’, ‘aw’, ‘au’ (as in for, saw, sauce) before ‘ore’ and ‘our’ (as in more, your).”

Choosing between graphemes when spelling is taught through orthographic strategies (Spelling, Stage 1). NESA's example: “words do not typically end with the grapheme oi but they can end with oy”. Students also identify plausible letter sequences: “the trigraph igh is plausible but ihg is not”.

Practice and apply

Blending to read

Students blend all through the word to decode, as in the content: “Blend single-letter grapheme–phoneme correspondences to decode VC and CVC words”.

Segmenting to spell

NESA: “They should be encouraged to orally segment words prior to writing them.” Students “write the spelling of a word, or select letters to spell a word, on a mini whiteboard and show the teacher before using the mini whiteboard for the next word”.

Manipulation

The oral manipulation skills from Phonological awareness, applied with letter tiles: ship → shop → chop → chip.

Modelled sentences and dictation

CEDoW routine The teacher thinks aloud while writing a sentence with taught GPCs. Students then write a dictated sentence and read it back to check. This applies the content “apply this knowledge when writing words and creating texts”.

Responding to spelling errors

Affirm what is correct and teach the convention. NESA: an instructional sequence “should also be used with reference to evidence gathered from systematic spelling error analysis”.

Longer words (Stage 1). NESA: “Students should be able to segment and blend all phonemes in one-syllable words before applying this strategy to multisyllabic words.” Content: “Blend and decode 2-syllable words with taught vowel graphs, digraphs, trigraphs and quadgraphs …”. CEDoW routine Look for morphemes, find the vowels, read each syllable, blend the syllables, then check the meaning.

Irregular high-frequency words

NESA, Spelling, Early Stage 1Content: “Combine phonological, phonic, orthographic and morphemic knowledge to spell taught high-frequency irregular words comprising up to 3 phonemes.” Advice: “Words should be taught by explaining the phonological, orthographic and morphological features as appropriate.”

NESA (Early Stage 1 additional advice): “Utilise high-frequency word sets that feature taught GPCs when modelling the process of blending and segmenting.”

NESA, Additional teaching advice, Reading fluency, Early Stage 1“Irregular high-frequency words should be taught explicitly, systematically and with regular practice. It is best to make lists of these words from texts being used in class.” Make them part of an interactive word wall and highlight “features that may contain regular GPCs and the irregular parts that students need to ‘learn by heart’.” For said, if students know the GPCs for s and d, “they will know that part of the word”, and hearts are drawn above the a and i.

CEDoW routine Say the word in a sentence, segment it orally, map each phoneme to its grapheme, mark the part to learn by heart, then read and write it. Revisit it in later lessons.

Independent practice and targeted instruction

Students practise independently while the teacher works with small groups or individuals on assessed needs HITS 10: Differentiated teaching.

NESA, Early Stage 1 teaching advice“The instruction should be targeted and may include small-group structures.” (Spelling). Students learning EAL/D “may need additional support with unknown phonemes” (Phonic knowledge). Some Aboriginal students “may need support to further develop code-switching between Aboriginal English and SAE in relation to spelling”; teachers should “be aware of the linguistic features of Aboriginal English and how these may influence spelling, such as fricatives – for example, them/dem, home/ome” (Spelling). “Decisions about adjustments for students with disability should be made in the context of collaborative curriculum planning.”
NESA, Additional teaching advice, Phonic knowledge, Early Stage 1“Additional opportunities for guided and independent practice may be required, either within the classroom or through specific interventions. Early intervention to support the development of literacy skills is essential.”
Students who are deaf or hard of hearing (NESA)

NESA advises that teaching “will vary according to the student’s” level of hearing loss, hearing technology and preferred mode of communication, and that teachers consider “what phonemes they can hear”. “Some students will require more intensive explicit instruction in phonemic awareness.” Strategies that can support students include:

  • reducing background noise inside and outside the classroom, and reducing echoes
  • making sure there is good lighting, and that the student can see your face clearly
  • using manipulatives
  • using visual supports and cues, such as pairing a sound with a particular hand shape or movement
  • recognising the impact on auditory memory for students who are deaf or hard of hearing and providing additional time for students to respond.

“Seeking advice from teachers or specialists with expertise in supporting students who are deaf or hard of hearing will assist in selecting appropriate strategies.”

NESA, Reading fluency, Early Stage 1“For Early Stage 1 students in the beginning stages of learning to read, the instructional focus is on the accuracy of reading skills, rather than the rate of reading. Early Stage 1 students commence with becoming automatic at identifying graphemes, then blending those graphemes to read words and phrases, moving on to reading decodable connected text. Once students can accurately and confidently read connected decodable text then a focus on rate and prosody is appropriate.”
NESA, Additional teaching advice, Phonic knowledge, Stage 1Consider “daily opportunities for students to practise taught content using decodable texts in conjunction with a growing repertoire of age-appropriate literature”. “Read aloud a wide range of texts to students still practising their phonic skills. Discuss examples of GPCs in words used by authors of those texts.”

Selecting decodable texts

NESA's Early Stage 1 additional advice: “Use decodable texts that students can apply and consolidate GPCs daily, until students have demonstrated mastery of GPCs.” NESA advises choosing decodable texts that:

  • have a clear sequence of GPCs that closely matches the sequence of instruction
  • progressively incorporate a variety of words which use the explicitly taught GPCs
  • promote blending by having approximately 80 per cent of the text which can be independently decoded using taught GPCs
  • use frequent graphemic representations of the target GPCs
  • systematically introduce high-frequency words
  • contain connected text and have correct grammar and punctuation
  • include a story or information that carries meaning
  • have appropriately sized text which is well-spaced across the page.
NESA, Phonic knowledge, Early Stage 1“As students learn new letter sound relationships, they will progress to more complex decodable texts that allow them to practise new and previously learnt GPCs. The use of decodable texts should not be protracted. Once students have mastered all components of the extended code, they should access natural language texts.”

Making meaning

NESA, Additional teaching advice, Reading comprehension, Early Stage 1Before reading, the teacher reviews the target GPCs and the irregular high-frequency words in the text, and unpacks new vocabulary. During reading, “The main focus is on the decoding of the text”: students chorally read each word of a sentence, the teacher models fluent reading of the sentence, and students re-read it chorally. After reading, “The teacher asks questions at the completion of the text.” “The pictures may provide additional information to support comprehension. However, pictures should not be used as a strategy to read unknown words. Students should be provided with support to use their blending skills to decode words.”

NESA advises supporting students to monitor their own reading by “alerting them to stop when they know they have lost meaning when reading”, “having them re-read and check if they make an error” and modelling questions such as “Did that make sense?”. In Stage 1, teachers support students to “self-correct when fluency is interrupted”. Reading literature aloud to students “helps lay the foundation for them to become good readers and writers” and “provides opportunities for teachers to model fluent reading”.

Oral reading

NESA: “Engaging students in oral reading can promote reading confidence and enjoyment, and improved reading fluency.” NESA's Early Stage 1 additional advice for Reading fluency describes these strategies:

Choral reading

“Have students read a selection of a decodable text in unison with you. Read at a moderate rate and instruct students to ‘keep your voice in time with me.’”

Echo reading

“Divide the class in half. One half reads a short passage or poem. The other half then mimics this reading.” (In the CEDoW model, the teacher can also read a sentence for students to echo.)

Paired reading

“Using the same text, 2 or 3 students work together to practise their oral reading.” Model how to give feedback, such as “‘I noticed that you stopped reading at the full stop.’” HITS 5: Collaborative learning

Repeated reading

“A student should practise reading a decodable passage or text several times aloud for fluency, with guidance and feedback on each read.”

Cloze reading

“Pause on target words or phrases and signal to the students to chorally read the indicated word(s).”

Listening to every reader

“Ideally the classroom teacher will listen to each student read a minimum of 3 times per week.” Give “goal-oriented (rather than effort-oriented) feedback”, such as “‘I heard you read all of the words in the text quickly and correctly’”.

8. Assessment: Checking the Code

Assessing phonic knowledge shows which GPCs each student can read and spell, so the next ones can be taught. NESA advises using the instructional sequence with reference to assessment data, including systematic spelling error analysis. This step adds practical suggestions.

What to Assess

AreaThe question it answersExample task
GPC knowledgeDoes the student know the phoneme for each taught grapheme?Show taught graphemes one at a time: “What sound does this make?”
DecodingCan the student blend GPCs to read words?Read a list of words using taught GPCs, from VC and CVC to longer and multisyllabic words.
Decoding unfamiliar wordsIs the student decoding, or relying on memory?Read pseudowords made from taught GPCs, such as vop or shend, which can't be recognised from memory.
EncodingCan the student segment words and choose the right graphemes?Dictate words and sentences using taught GPCs, and check each grapheme.
Application in textsDoes the student apply the code when reading and writing?Listen to the student read a decodable text, and look at taught GPCs in their writing.

The areas follow the syllabus content and NESA's teaching advice. The tasks are this module's suggestions.

Ways to Assess Phonic Knowledge

Cumulative GPC checks

  • Check every taught GPC regularly, not just the latest ones.
  • Record which are secure, so review can target gaps.

Word and pseudoword reading

  • Real words show application; pseudowords show whether students can decode words they have never seen.
  • Phonics screening checks commonly use both.

Dictation

  • Dictate words, then a short sentence, using taught GPCs.
  • Dictation shows encoding in the same sequence as decoding.

Reading decodable texts

  • Listen to students read a text matched to their taught GPCs, and note accuracy and how they tackle unfamiliar words.
  • This tells you whether a student is ready for the next text, or for natural language texts.

Spelling error analysis

  • Look at errors in everyday writing: is every phoneme represented, and with a plausible grapheme?
  • NESA advises using evidence from systematic spelling error analysis alongside any instructional sequence.

Checking in later stages

  • When students in Stages 2 and 3 misread or misspell words, check which GPCs are not yet secure.
  • NESA's Stage 2 advice is that intervention in phonological awareness and/or phonic knowledge will then be needed.

Assessing Across the Stages

Assessment works best when it is tied to the content students are learning. For example:

StageContent point (NESA)Assessment idea

Early Stage 1Segment and encode CVC words containing consonant digraphsDictate words such as ship, chat and moth, and check each grapheme.
Stage 1Blend and decode one-syllable words with taught extended vowel graphs and digraphs, including graphemes for r-controlled vowels and diphthongs, and apply this when reading textsStudents read words and pseudowords with taught graphemes such as ur, ir, er and ou.
Stage 1Decode 2-syllable base words with common double consonants when reading textsStudents read words such as rabbit, kitten and puppet, and show where they split them.

Principles for Assessing Phonic Knowledge

  • Assess what has been taught. Match items to the GPCs in the instructional sequence.
  • Assess both directions. Check decoding and encoding for the same GPCs.
  • Include pseudowords. NESA: non-words “do not carry meaning but contain GPCs that are plausible (eg drot, shann)” (Early Stage 1; Stage 1 example: shrump), and can be used to assess whether a student knows taught GPCs and “how to blend and/or segment a sequence of phonemes”.
  • Track irregular words. NESA: “Prepare an extended list of the irregular high-frequency words that students have been taught and record if they are read correctly and automatically. Date these assessment episodes and consider progress over time.”
  • Assess often and briefly. Quick, cumulative checks keep the pace right for each student.
  • Assess individually. Group reading can hide students who are not yet decoding.
  • Use the evidence. Feed what you learn into the next OCI cycle (Step 6): which GPCs to review, who to regroup, and who is ready to move on.

Key message: assess the GPCs you have taught, in both directions, with real words and pseudowords, and use the results to set the pace for each student.
