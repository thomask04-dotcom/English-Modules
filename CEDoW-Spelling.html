Spelling

1. Spelling: What It Is and Why It Matters

NESA describes spelling as an essential word-formation process when creating written texts. Spelling proficiency in the first year of school is important for writing and reading development, and learning to spell is important for expanding vocabulary. In the NSW English K–10 Syllabus (2022), Spelling is a focus area with an outcome in every stage. In the Writing Rope, spelling is part of the Transcription strand (Sedita 2019), and it is taught alongside phonic knowledge, which connects it to Word Recognition in the Reading Rope (Scarborough 2001).

Spelling Draws on Several Kinds of Knowledge

To spell words, students combine knowledge of speech sounds (phonology), letter patterns and generalisations (orthography), and meaningful word parts and how they connect (morphology). From Stage 1, NESA adds how many words derive from different languages (etymology).

Common Misconceptions

Spelling is learnt by memorising lists

NESA states that spelling should not be taught through visual memorisation of whole words and repetitive drill. Explicit instruction in spelling is essential, and words for teaching should follow a common pattern or generalisation.

Spelling is just sounding out

Segmenting phonemes is one strategy, but English spelling also depends on letter patterns, positional rules, meaningful word parts and word origins. The -ed in jumped sounds like /t/ but is spelt ed because it marks past tense.

Summarised from NESA (2022), English K–10 Syllabus support: Teaching advice for Spelling (Early Stage 1 to Stage 3), which cites Abbott et al. (2010), Sumner et al. (2016), Conrad et al. (2019) and Berninger and Fayol (2008), and the syllabus content for Spelling.

Key message: spelling combines knowledge of sounds, letter patterns, word parts and word origins. It is taught explicitly in every stage, not memorised, because it frees students to write and strengthens their reading and vocabulary.

3. How Learning Builds Within a Stage

Three components, every stage

Every stage has a Phonological component, an Orthographic component and a Morphological component. Early Stage 1 also has Integrated spelling components, where students combine all their knowledge to spell taught high-frequency irregular words. NESA advises explicit teaching of all three components.

From applying to explaining and justifying

The outcome verbs grow across the stages: students apply generalisations and strategies in K–2, select, apply and describe them in Stage 2, and automatically apply them and justify the strategies they use for unfamiliar words in Stage 3. More content points ask students to explain, so knowledge of how spelling works becomes explicit.

4. Pedagogy: The Evidence Base

This step sets out what the evidence base of the syllabus says about teaching spelling: NESA's teaching advice, and the research behind it.

What the Evidence Base Says

Explicit teaching: every student's entitlement

NESA states that explicit instruction in spelling is essential, and that increasing explicit instruction can lead to sustained improvements in spelling, as well as writing and reading more broadly. An evidence-based approach involves explicit teaching of the phonological, orthographic and morphological components of spelling, and spelling should not be taught through visual memorisation of whole words and repetitive drill.

The syllabus content for Spelling is part of every student's learning entitlement. Explicit teaching guarantees equity: every student is taught how English spelling works, rather than left to memorise words one at a time.

Summarised from NESA (2022), English K–10 Syllabus support: Teaching advice for Spelling (Early Stage 1 to Stage 3). Describing the syllabus content as a learning entitlement is this module's framing.

NESA's teaching advice has six key messages:

1. Teach the components explicitly, targeted to need

  • Explicitly teach the phonological, orthographic and morphological components. Ideally, instruction is targeted and in small groups.
  • Embed spontaneous revision of taught concepts as opportunities arise.

2. Choose words carefully

  • Select words students have learnt to decode, which follow a common orthographic or morphological pattern or generalisation.
  • Teach each word's meaning and how to read and spell it, explaining its phonological, orthographic and morphological features.

3. Phonological strategies

  • Teach syllabification and phoneme segmenting.
  • Students should be able to segment and blend all phonemes in one-syllable words before applying this to multisyllabic words, and orally segment words before writing them.

4. Orthographic strategies

  • Teach positional constraints (words don't typically end with oi, but can end with oy) and plausible letter sequences (igh, not ihg).
  • Teach how to use a dictionary and spell-checking resources, contractions, and word-specific knowledge for words with infrequent graphemes.

5. Morphological strategies and etymology

  • Teach the meaning and function of morphemes and the rules for combining them to produce words.
  • Explore etymology, including words borrowed from Aboriginal languages, such as gugubarra from Wiradjuri, represented in English as kookaburra.

6. Base teaching on evidence

  • Use the suggested instructional sequences, which align with Phonic knowledge in K–2, with reference to evidence from systematic spelling error analysis.
  • Link the teaching of spelling and vocabulary closely.
Aboriginal English and Standard Australian English. Some Aboriginal students may need support to further develop code-switching between Aboriginal English and Standard Australian English in spelling. Be aware of linguistic features of Aboriginal English that may influence spelling, such as fricatives (them/dem, home/ome), and of culturally specific terms and words from Aboriginal languages, which should not be ‘corrected’ when used contextually, such as Blak, brudda, tidda and gammon.

What the Research Behind the Syllabus Says

Spelling and writing

  • Spelling proficiency influences how effectively a student creates written texts (Hayes and Berninger 2014).
  • Spelling is the essential bridge between idea generation and text creation (Abbott et al. 2010).
  • Spelling ability can compromise the precision and variety of words used when writing (Sumner et al. 2016).

Teaching spelling

  • Spelling words, rather than just reading them, helps establish word-specific memory and supports word reading efficiency (Conrad et al. 2019).
  • A meta-analysis of 53 studies found formal spelling instruction improved spelling, with gains maintained over time and generalised to writing, and also improved phonological awareness and reading (Graham and Santangelo 2014).
  • Spelling should not be taught through visual memorisation of whole words and repetitive drill (Berninger and Fayol 2008).

These studies are cited in NESA (2022), Teaching advice for Spelling (Stages 2 and 3).

Key message: teach the phonological, orthographic and morphological components explicitly, with carefully chosen words that follow a pattern, link spelling to vocabulary, and target teaching using spelling error analysis.

7. Instructional Model: Teaching Spelling

The CEDoW Spelling Instructional Model uses the same three-phase lesson as the phonics model: review, explicit teaching, and application and transfer. What is different is the week. Lessons are organised across the week so that the phonological, orthographic and morphological components of spelling are each taught explicitly, then connected and consolidated.

NESA boxes quote NESA's syllabus content and teaching advice, the primary source. CEDoW routine marks a CEDoW routine that puts that advice into practice.

Why Coordinate Three Components?

NESA, Spelling, Early Stage 1To spell, students combine knowledge of “speech sounds (phonology)”, “letter patterns and generalisations (orthography)” and “meaningful word parts and how they can connect (morphology)”. “An evidence-based instructional approach involves explicit teaching of the phonological, orthographic and morphological components of spelling.”

Phonological

Students use phonic knowledge to record graphemes after hearing and segmenting a spoken word. Phonology is a critical foundation, but it cannot be relied on alone to spell every word in Standard Australian English.

Orthographic

Students learn the conventional letter patterns of English, including how the position of a phoneme in a word influences which grapheme is used, and recognise when a spelling looks correct or incorrect.

Morphological

Students learn how morphemes, the smallest meaningful units of language, combine to form words and change to express grammatical meaning. This moves spelling beyond sound towards meaning.

The research behind the week. Triple Word Form Theory proposes that, with explicit teaching, children can learn to use the phonological, orthographic and morphological components together from the early years of learning to write, and that these integrate with increasing accuracy and efficiency over time (Daffern, 2017). This is why every stage, from Early Stage 1, has content in all three components, and why the week teaches each one explicitly and then coordinates them.

The emphasis shifts across K–6. In K–2, students develop foundational knowledge and skill, which they apply with increasing accuracy and automaticity when writing. In Years 3–6, NESA advises, “Spelling instruction in Years 3 to 6 enables students to build their vocabulary knowledge and refine linguistic understandings of word structures.”

Inside Each Lesson

Whatever the weekly structure, every spelling lesson uses the same model as phonics. Use the K–2 version in Early Stage 1 and Stage 1, and the 3–6 version in Stages 2 and 3. Checking for understanding and feedback run through every phase, so every student responds and the teacher sees who has it.

Choose a phase to see what it looks like in a spelling lesson. The High Impact Teaching Strategies (HITS) are named where they matter most.

Activate prior knowledge

Brief, fast-paced retrieval of previously taught generalisations and words HITS 6: Multiple exposures. Students say each word, use it in a sentence and explain what they know about its spelling. NESA: “Encourage students to say and write target spelling words in the context of sentences.”

NESA, Spelling, Early Stage 1“Words for teaching spelling should be carefully selected using words students have learnt to decode. Words should follow a common pattern or generalisation; these may be orthographic or morphological.”

Handwriting

Students handwrite the words they are learning to spell. In K–2 this is letter formation taught alongside phonics and spelling; in Years 3–6 it builds fluency, and NESA notes that the action of handwriting supports memory for word-specific spelling.

K–2: phonological awareness and revision

In Early Stage 1 and Stage 1, review also includes oral segmenting and phoneme, word and sentence level revision, as in the K–2 version of the model.

Learning intention and success criteria

State the day's focus as a learning intention with success criteria HITS 1: Setting goals, for example: “We are learning that we usually double the final consonant of a short-vowel base word before adding -ing.”

Explicit teaching

NESA, Spelling, Stages 2 and 3“Explicit instruction in spelling is essential. Increasing explicit instruction can lead to sustained improvements in spelling, as well as writing and reading more broadly (Graham et al. 2012; Graham and Santangelo 2014). Spelling should not be taught through visual memorisation of whole words and repetitive drill (Berninger and Fayol 2008).”

Teach the generalisation explicitly using the metalanguage HITS 3: Explicit teaching, then model it with a worked example, thinking aloud about each component HITS 4: Worked examples. Ask questions that make students apply the generalisation, such as “Why does catch end in tch?” HITS 7: Questioning.

Metalanguage

NESA: “Teachers should introduce and use metalanguage when explicitly teaching the linguistic features of words.” Early Stage 1 metalanguage may include letter name, phoneme, grapheme, syllable, vowel, consonant, morpheme, base word, suffix and prefix. Stage 1 adds digraph, trigraph, split digraph (“sometimes referred to as ‘silent final e’”), and base word vs root. “Students should be provided with opportunities to imitate the spelling processes that the teacher models.”

Practice and apply

CEDoW routine Students practise with the teacher on mini whiteboards and with word cards, with immediate feedback: sorting words by pattern, building words from morphemes, and writing dictated words and sentences after segmenting them orally HITS 8: Feedback.

Independent practice and targeted instruction

Students apply the generalisation independently while the teacher works with small groups on assessed needs HITS 10: Differentiated teaching.

NESA, Spelling teaching advice“Ideally the instruction is targeted and in small-groups.” (Spelling, Stage 1) “Some students may find the link between phonemes and graphemes more difficult to establish and will need extra time and repetition to consolidate these skills.” (Spelling, Stages 2 and 3)

Students apply the learning in connected writing, and notice it in reading, across the curriculum. They proofread their own writing for the taught pattern and explain their spelling choices HITS 9: Metacognitive strategies.

NESA, Spelling, Stages 2 and 3“Spelling is the essential bridge between idea generation and text creation (Abbott et al. 2010). If too much attention is placed on spelling while creating texts, it can be difficult to devote cognitive attention to other important aspects of writing.”
NESA, Additional teaching advice, Spelling, Stage 1“The teaching of spelling may occur in parallel with reading and writing instruction. For example, students reading a text may examine the linguistic properties of specific words used in the context of sentences. This can be particularly helpful when focusing on the morphological component of spelling.”

Partners can check each other's writing for the week's pattern and explain the generalisation HITS 5: Collaborative learning. Spelling errors in writing are analysed by component, phonological, orthographic or morphological, to decide what to teach next (Step 8).

8. Assessment: Reading the Errors

Spelling errors are evidence of what students know. NESA advises that any instructional sequence be used with reference to evidence from assessment data, including systematic spelling error analysis. This step shows how to analyse errors by component, and adds practical suggestions.

Analyse Errors by Component

ComponentExample errorWhat it suggests
Phonologicaljup for jump; choclat for chocolateA phoneme or syllable has been missed: the student may need to segment words more carefully, orally first.
Orthographicboi for boy; ckat for catA plausible grapheme in the wrong position: the student may need teaching on positional constraints or letter patterns.
Morphologicaljumpt for jumped; hopeing for hopingA word part spelt by sound, or a suffix convention missed: the student may need teaching on morphemes and suffix rules.
Etymologicalskool for schoolA plausible spelling that doesn't reflect the word's origin: the student may need word-specific or etymology teaching.

The components are from the syllabus and NESA's teaching advice. The examples and interpretations are this module's suggestions.

Ways to Assess Spelling

Error analysis of everyday writing

  • Collect misspellings from students' writing across subjects and sort them by component.
  • Look for patterns, not single errors.

Targeted dictation

  • Dictate words, and then sentences, that test a specific generalisation, such as suffixes that need a change to the base word.
  • Include words students haven't practised, to check transfer.

Spelling inventories

  • Graded word lists that sample features from simple to complex can show which features each student has mastered.
  • Use them at the start of a unit or term to group students.

Explaining strategies

  • Ask students to explain how they spelt a word, or why a word is spelt as it is.
  • The Stage 2 outcome asks students to describe strategies, and Stage 3 to justify them.

Proofreading tasks

  • Give students a short text with errors to find and correct, and observe how they use reference tools.
  • From Stage 2, proofreading is syllabus content.

Transfer to writing

  • Check whether taught generalisations appear correctly in students' own writing, weeks after teaching.
  • NESA's Stage 3 outcome is about applying generalisations automatically.

Assessing Across the Stages

Assessment works best when it is tied to the content students are learning. For example:

StageContent point (NESA)Assessment idea
Early Stage 1Know that the digraphs zz, ss, ll, ff and ck do not usually start a word in Standard Australian EnglishDictate words such as kick, bell and cat, and check where students place ck, ll and c.
Stage 1Use spelling conventions when adding tense-marking suffixesDictate jumped, hopped and liked, and ask students to explain what happened to each base word.
Stage 2Understand that the schwa occurs in an unstressed syllable and apply this knowledge when spellingStudents clap words such as pencil and button, find the unstressed syllable, and spell it.
Stage 3Explain and use spelling conventions for assimilated prefixes such as in-, ad-, com-Students spell illegal, immature and correct, and explain how the prefix changed.
From NESA's additional teaching advice. “Instructional planning should be informed by ongoing and systematic monitoring of spelling errors.” “Where possible, use reliable, standardised and/or norm-referenced dictation assessments based on Australian norms. These should include scope for spelling error analysis. Such assessments should also be combined with analyses of words that students spell when creating their own written texts. This will allow checking for generalisation into less controlled writing situations.” In spontaneous writing, “errors should be analysed to see if they reflect difficulties with phonological, orthographic and morphological processing.” “Using error-analysis data to inform teaching priorities in these components of spelling is an effective practice.” (Additional teaching advice, Spelling, Early Stage 1 and Stage 1)

Principles for Assessing Spelling

  • Analyse, don't just count. The kind of error matters more than the number of errors.
  • Assess in real writing. Spelling tests show what students can do when they focus on spelling; writing shows what they do automatically.
  • Test generalisations, not lists. Include unpractised words that follow the taught pattern.
  • Ask students to explain. Explaining and justifying strategies is part of the outcomes from Stage 2.
  • Respect students' languages. Don't treat culturally specific words used in context as errors.
  • Use the evidence. Feed what you learn into the next OCI cycle (Step 6): which generalisations to teach, and to which groups.

Key message: assess spelling by analysing errors by component, test generalisations with unpractised words, and check that taught patterns transfer to students' everyday writing.
