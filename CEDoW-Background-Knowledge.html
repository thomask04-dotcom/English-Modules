Building Background Knowledge for Reading and Writing

1. What Background Knowledge Is

Background knowledge is everything a student already knows that they bring to a new text or task: knowledge of the world, of topics and subjects, of words, and of how texts work. It is stored in long-term memory, and it is what readers draw on to make sense of what they read, and what writers draw on to have something to say.

NESA, Teaching advice, Reading comprehension, Stages 2 and 3To understand texts, proficient readers draw on: background knowledge; vocabulary knowledge; knowledge of text structures. “They make connections to support their understanding by integrating new information with old information while reading.”

Background Knowledge and the Mental Model

NESA, Teaching advice, Reading comprehension, Early Stage 1“To gain meaning, a reader needs to construct a representation, or mental model, for the text they’re reading.” “In Early Stage 1, students will most frequently develop their mental models of texts by drawing on their personal experiences, rather than from other influences.” “If they have never been to a farm, they will find it difficult to construct a mental model of a farm to support their comprehension of the text.”
Prior knowledge or background knowledge? The two terms are often used interchangeably. In this module, prior knowledge means what a particular student already knows before a lesson, and background knowledge means the broader body of knowledge that teaching sets out to build over time. The syllabus uses background knowledge; NESA's teaching advice uses both.

Key message: background knowledge is what students already know about the world, topics, words and texts. It is the raw material of comprehension and the source of ideas for writing.

Next: Step 2 explains why knowledge matters for all learning, and what a knowledge-rich approach to curriculum design means.

2. Why Background Knowledge Matters

Knowledge Makes New Learning Easier

Working memory is limited

Students can only process a small amount of new information at once. Knowledge held securely in long-term memory can be recalled with little effort, which frees working memory for understanding, reasoning and creating.

AERO (2024)

Knowledge sticks to knowledge

The more students already know about a topic, the easier it is to take in and remember related new knowledge. AERO likens prior knowledge to Velcro: new knowledge sticks to what is already there.

AERO (2024)

Skills depend on knowledge

Thinking skills such as inferring, comparing and evaluating cannot be applied in a vacuum: students infer, compare and evaluate something. Knowledge helps at every stage of learning, as information is taken in, thought about and stored (Willingham, 2006).

AERO (2024); Willingham (2006)

Knowledgewhat students already know
→
Comprehensionreading and listening with understanding
→
New learningwhich adds to what students know

Knowledge begets knowledge: each well-understood text adds to the knowledge students bring to the next.

A Knowledge-Rich Approach to Curriculum Design

As part of NSW curriculum reform, NESA commissioned the Australian Education Research Organisation (AERO) to review the evidence for a knowledge-rich curriculum. AERO describes a knowledge-rich curriculum as one that prioritises and explicitly sets out the subject knowledge and related skills students should learn at each stage, and identifies four design features:

Selective

Content is chosen purposefully for each subject.

Coherent

Content is connected across topics, subjects and stages, so knowledge in one area supports learning in another.

Carefully sequenced

Content builds on prior learning and increases in complexity, so deep and broad knowledge grows over time.

Clear

The curriculum states explicitly what students are expected to know, understand and be able to do.

Summarised from AERO (2024), A knowledge-rich approach to curriculum design.

Building Knowledge Across the Curriculum

English is where students learn to read and write, but it is also where they read and write about something. When English units are connected to the knowledge students are building in Science and Technology, HSIE and the other learning areas, each strengthens the other.

NESA, Teaching advice, Stages 1 to 3“Students in Stages 2 and 3 continue to build understanding of increasingly complex texts so that they can enjoy texts and learn across all subjects. For example, in Science, students read to learn facts on natural phenomena.” In Stage 1, through understanding and responding to literature, students can “build disciplinary and world knowledge” and “broaden their vocabulary and grammar.”

Integrated literacy and content

A meta-analysis of 35 studies (K–5) found that integrating literacy with science and social studies instruction improved vocabulary and comprehension, while also building content knowledge.

Hwang, Cabell and Joyner (2022)

Knowledge-building in the early years

In a first-grade trial, a 10-day thematic science unit improved science knowledge, vocabulary and reading comprehension, without widening gaps between students.

Kim et al. (2021)

An equity issue

Students who struggle with reading tend to have gaps in knowledge. A curriculum that deliberately builds shared knowledge gives every student access to it, whatever they bring from home.

AERO (2024)

What it looks like in English, and beyond

This module shows what building background knowledge looks like in English, through reading and writing. But the same strategies apply across the whole curriculum: building the field, teaching topic vocabulary explicitly, reading connected texts, talking and writing about content. They work in Science and Technology, HSIE, PDHPE and Creative Arts in the same way, and every learning area is an opportunity to build the knowledge students bring to their reading and writing.

NESA, Teaching advice (additional): Teaching vocabulary to English language learners K–6“Vocabulary development is essential to support student learning across the primary curriculum.” “It also supports students to build schemas and make connections with their world. Understanding vocabulary is necessary for students to access the curriculum. All students benefit from explicit vocabulary instruction.”

Key message: knowledge makes new learning easier, and a knowledge-rich, coherent and well-sequenced curriculum lets knowledge accumulate across subjects and years, for every student.

What Changes Across the Stages

Early Stage 1

Students use background knowledge, mostly from personal experience, to make connections and understand characters.

Stage 1

Students activate background knowledge and recognise when they need more of it to make an inference.

Stage 2

Students build topic knowledge as well as activating it, and notice how it shapes their mental model. Writers research topics.

Stage 3

Students bring subject and conceptual knowledge to new tasks, analyse how it works with language and inferencing, and synthesise across texts.

Read to every day. The syllabus text requirements ask teachers to give students daily opportunities to be read to. NESA explains: “By reading aloud and engaging students in discussions, teachers provide students with access to texts beyond their immediate means, introducing them to new ideas and vocabulary and encouraging them to explore different ways of thinking.”

Key message: the syllabus moves students from using what they know, to activating it, to deliberately building and analysing knowledge for reading and writing.

4. How Background Knowledge Supports Reading and Writing

Reading

NESA, Teaching advice, Reading comprehension and Oral language“Inferences rely on background knowledge and can relate to knowledge of the words encountered in the text or knowledge of the particular type of text and other connections that the reader makes.” (Early Stage 1) “Background knowledge supports reading comprehension.” (Stages 2 and 3)

It fills the gaps

Writers leave out what they assume readers know. Comprehension depends on filling these gaps with correct inferences, and inferences depend on knowledge (Willingham, 2006).

Willingham (2006); NESA

It can outweigh reading skill

In a classic study, students who knew a lot about baseball understood and recalled a baseball text better than those who knew little, whatever their reading ability. Weaker readers with high knowledge did as well as stronger readers with high knowledge.

Recht and Leslie (1988)

Its effect depends on the text

A review of 23 studies found that background knowledge has a range of effects, shaped by the text, the mental model it requires and any misconceptions the reader holds. Readers with less knowledge benefited more from highly cohesive texts.

Smith et al. (2021)

It is part of language comprehension

In Scarborough's Reading Rope, background knowledge is one of the strands of language comprehension, woven together with word recognition to produce skilled reading.

Scarborough (2001)

Knowledge is necessary, not sufficient. Knowledge does not replace decoding or language skills. NESA notes that “Although word reading is necessary for reading comprehension, it is not sufficient (Oakhill et al. 2014).” Strong readers need both secure word reading and rich knowledge.

Writing

NESA, Teaching advice, Creating written texts, Stages 2 and 3Proficient writers “apply knowledge of a topic and its related vocabulary, and combine these with an understanding of genre when developing ideas for writing”. Even at single sentence level, writing requires a student to “generate an idea”, “retrieve the words that represent it” and “arrange and rearrange the words mentally into a sequence that makes sense syntactically and semantically.”

Something to say

Students cannot write well about what they do not know. Topic knowledge supplies the ideas, details and precise vocabulary that make writing informative and convincing.

More room to write well

Writing places heavy demands on working memory. Writers with extensive topic knowledge can draw on long-term memory, leaving more capacity for planning, sentence construction and revising.

McCutchen (2011); NESA

Knowledge of genre

Knowing how texts work, gained by reading and analysing mentor texts, helps students structure their own writing for purpose and audience.

AERO (2024); NESA

NESA, Teaching advice, Creating written texts, Stage 1Stage 1: “As students progress into Stage 1, writing contexts should cover both personal experience and content they are learning about in other areas of the curriculum.” Teachers “offer opportunities for students to collaboratively build their background knowledge in preparation for writing for a particular context, purpose and audience”.

Reading, Writing and Knowledge Feed Each Other

Reading and listeningbuild knowledge and vocabulary
⇄
Knowledgeideas, words and concepts
⇄
Writing and talkinguse and consolidate knowledge
NESA, Teaching advice, Stages 2 and 3“Writing can strengthen the process of acquiring new knowledge across different subjects and schooling years (Graham et al. 2020; Huerta and Garza 2019).” “Writing about material that has been read can also improve comprehension of that material (Graham and Hebert 2011).” “Oral language supports vocabulary learning and builds background knowledge.”

Key message: knowledge lets readers make the inferences texts demand, and gives writers something to say and the words to say it. Reading, talking and writing about a topic all build knowledge in turn.

5. Activating Prior Knowledge vs Building Knowledge

Both matter, but they are not the same. Activating brings to mind knowledge students already have. Building deliberately teaches knowledge students do not yet have. Activation only works when there is knowledge to activate.

NESA, Teaching advice (additional): Teaching vocabulary to English language learners K–6“Building the field refers to pre-teaching vocabulary and developing background knowledge to enable students to access and participate in learning.” “It is important that building the field activities are interactive so that students can use, observe and experience the language associated with the topic.”
NESA, Teaching advice, Reading comprehension, Early Stage 1“Teachers can activate background knowledge that the students already have prior to reading and build background knowledge when students do not have the experiences or vocabulary to relate to the text.”
NESA, Syllabus and teaching adviceStage 2 content: “Build topic knowledge, including key vocabulary, and activate background knowledge prior to and during reading”. Stage 1 advice: “Students experiencing difficulties with comprehension may benefit from explicitly building the field of background knowledge before reading a text.”
Activating prior knowledgeBuilding knowledge
PurposeBring relevant knowledge to mind so students connect it to the new text or task.Teach the knowledge and vocabulary students need, so they have something to connect to.
AssumesStudents already know something useful about the topic.Students may know little, or hold misconceptions.
TimingMinutes, at the start of a lesson or before and during reading.Over days and weeks, across a unit, and across years as the curriculum accumulates.
PlanningA lesson routine.A curriculum decision: which topics, which texts, in what sequence.
Risk if used aloneStudents with little knowledge have little to activate, so gaps widen. Misconceptions may be reinforced.Without connections to what students know, new knowledge may stay inert. Build and activate together.
Example“What do you already know about frogs?” before reading a frog life cycle text.A two-week unit reading a set of texts on life cycles, with explicit vocabulary, diagrams and writing to explain.

Why activation is not enough. Comprehension strategies such as activating prior knowledge are useful, but Willingham argues their benefits come from brief instruction and cannot substitute for knowledge itself (Willingham, 2006/07). Students who start with less knowledge gain least from activation and most from building, which is why knowledge-building is an equity strategy.

Key message: activate when students know something; build when they do not. Most units need both, and building is a planned, cumulative, curriculum-level decision.

6. Strategies for Activating and Building Knowledge

These strategies are shown in English, but they apply in every learning area. The fourth set shows NESA's examples from Science and Technology, HSIE, PDHPE and Creative Arts.

☞ Choose a set of strategies.

Use when students already know something about the topic. Keep it brief, and listen for gaps and misconceptions.

Preview the text

Look at the title, images and features together, and ask what students think the text will be about. In Early Stage 1, NESA suggests asking “guiding questions prior to their reading, eg ‘Do you think you know what this story will be about?’” and engaging students “in discussion about their text predictions.”

NESA, Reading comprehension, Early Stage 1

Quick retrieval

Start with a few low-stakes questions about a previous lesson or unit, so students recall what they know before new learning connects to it.

HITS: Multiple exposures

Know, Want to know, Learned

A thinking structure for connecting what students know with what they will learn. NESA lists “thinking structures such as Know – Want to know – Learned” among supports for background knowledge. Only use the Know column if students genuinely know something.

NESA, Reading comprehension, Stages 2 and 3

Make connections

Prompt students to “make text-to-self, text-to-text and text-to-world connections”, and to explain how the connection helps them understand.

NESA, Reading comprehension, Stage 1

Think-pair-share

Students tell a partner what they know about the topic, then share with the class. Listen for misconceptions to address before reading.

Support oral language for predicting

Predicting “requires the use of conditional language structures”, which can be challenging for EAL/D students. Provide “multiple oral models for a range of sentence and language structures”.

NESA, Reading comprehension, Early Stage 1

Use when students need knowledge they do not yet have. Plan these across a unit, not just a lesson.

Read sets of connected texts

Read several texts on the same topic over days or weeks, moving from simpler to more complex. Conceptually connected texts build knowledge and vocabulary more than unrelated texts.

Cervetti, Wright and Hwang (2016); Wright et al. (2022)

Read aloud texts beyond students’ reading level

“Students benefit from their teacher reading literature to them that is more complex in structure, language and visual elements than the texts they may read independently.” Include informational texts.

NESA, Understanding and responding to literature, Early Stage 1

Build the field before reading

Explicitly teach the essential concepts, context and vocabulary before students read. NESA: “It is necessary to pre-teach unfamiliar or challenging vocabulary prior to reading.”

NESA, Reading comprehension, Early Stage 1

Teach topic vocabulary explicitly

Select target words from the texts and learning-area topics students are studying. NESA: target words “can be selected from texts read to the students or concepts from Mathematics and other learning areas.” Plan for repeated encounters across the unit.

NESA, additional advice, Vocabulary, Early Stage 1

Connect English to learning-area units

Choose texts that teach about the topics students are learning in Science and Technology, HSIE and the Arts. NESA's example: a decodable text about a duck links to a previous unit on animal habitats when students describe “the duck’s habitat”.

Hwang et al. (2022); NESA, additional advice, Reading comprehension

Use concrete experiences and visuals

Excursions, objects, images and video give students experiences to build on. For EAL/D students, “Providing visuals or real-life objects for key words in a text will help EAL/D students connect meaning.”

NESA, Reading comprehension, Early Stage 1

Talk about the topic

Structured discussion, rich talk and yarning circles let students use new words and ideas. “Oral language supports vocabulary learning and builds background knowledge.”

NESA, Oral language, Stages 2 and 3

Write to learn

Have students summarise, explain and record what they have learnt about a topic. Writing about content reliably enhanced learning across subjects.

Graham, Kiuhara and MacKay (2020)

Organise and revisit knowledge

Use concept maps, text organisers and concept posters to show how ideas connect, and revisit key knowledge regularly so it is retained. NESA lists “concept posters, text organisers” among supports.

NESA, Reading comprehension, Stages 2 and 3; HITS: Multiple exposures

Sequence for accumulation

Plan units so that knowledge builds over a term and across years: later topics should draw on earlier ones. This is the coherent, carefully sequenced curriculum AERO describes.

AERO (2024)

Students write best about what they know.

Build knowledge before writing

Plan a knowledge-building phase before students write: read, view, discuss and take notes on the topic. In Stage 2, students “Research, summarise and list topic-related ideas when planning”.

Use mentor texts

Analyse mentor texts to build knowledge of genre and language features. NESA: mentor texts “provide models for consolidating knowledge about different kinds of texts, sentence types, grammatical usage, vocabulary choice and punctuation.”

NESA, Creating written texts, Early Stage 1

Co-construct word banks

Build topic word banks with students as knowledge grows. NESA advises “co-constructing and using word banks to support students’ language choices when writing”.

NESA, Creating written texts, Stages 2 and 3
